## 2. Flash is not the full model (NON-TEST)

**VO:**
Before any clips, a sorting rule. Most "Kling 4.0" videos from late September are actually Kling 4.0 Flash, a lighter early version. Flash tops out at 20 seconds and 720p, and it only takes a first frame.

The full model runs 3 to 30 seconds, accepts up to 10 keyframes and up to 15 reference files, and renders at 1080p. Kling lists 4K HDR as coming soon.

So if a clip runs past 20 seconds, uses keyframes, or is labelled 1080p, it isn't Flash. Everything in this video passed that check, or I'll flag it.

Kling makes three bigger claims, and I'll treat them as claims to test. One: longer clips give "emotional beats" room to develop without feeling rushed. Two: keyframes placed anywhere on the timeline guide shot changes, character entrances and reveals. Three: up to 15 references keep characters and products consistent. Kling also lists video extension to two minutes as "coming soon". Nobody can test that yet, so it's out of this video.

## 3. Letting it run for 30 seconds

**VO:**
The simplest test is to write a story and press go. EndFolding79421 did exactly that on X, using a long, novel-style prompt about a waitress named Lucy walking home at night and letting it run the full 30 seconds. In the frames we sampled she stays the same person, shot to shot.

Dheepanratnam went further, 39 minutes after launch. Their prompt is a timed beat sheet: a London street interview, an old woman with a lavender beehive, a black cat under her arm, every line pinned to a second. The dialogue lands roughly in order. One line arrives about four seconds early. Two action beats, her lowering her glasses and the cat switching off the mic, don't show up in the frames we checked.

Matt Starky tried a 30-second UGC ad. His screen reads "VIDEO 4.0 Preview, 1080p, 30s". That was a pre-release build. His presenter looks consistent in the frames we saw. What failed off camera, we don't know.

Three single takes, three different kinds of prompt: a short story, a timed script, an ad brief. In all three, the person holds up in the frames we sampled. Precise timing is less reliable.

## 4. The stitching claim vs what creators actually generated

**VO:**
Starky puts the promise plainly. Thirty seconds is "a full UGC ad in one generation."

Now look at what the most polished early work used. TechHalla's Cadillacs and Dinosaurs trailer is the strongest consistency showcase I found. Six references in one scene, four heroes, a punk gang, an explosion, all on-model. TechHalla's own prompt cards say that clip ran eight seconds. The fight clip, five hard cuts, ran seven.

AiHustleGame, a Kling creative partner, built a headphone ad out of 10-second shots. He regenerated whenever a character drifted, then trimmed in Premiere. In his words, "a 10 second generation does not mean I need all 10 seconds." From his video, I can't confirm he was on the full model rather than Flash.

Oprelia AI's review is the most thorough test I found. Every test in it ran between 10 and 20 seconds.

So the 30-second ceiling is real. The people getting the cleanest results mostly aren't using it.

## 5. Keyframes as story beats

**VO:**
Keyframes are the feature that should matter most for story. You pin images to the timeline and the model has to pass through them.

In Oprelia's test, the interface shows first, middle and last frame slots. Oprelia's first keyframe test, a clockwork bird, went wrong: the start is broken and one middle frame never appears. Oprelia still calls keyframes "the most amount of control right now over the model."

Then came the keyframed fight, a human boxer against an alien, built from seven reference frames in 19 seconds. Oprelia calls it "by far the best video out of the bunch."

On X, marco_a_b_c used four keyframes across a 30-second Halloween short, and AIWarper packed ten into ten seconds. Neither shared their keyframe images. Whether the beats landed where they meant them to, we can't tell.

TechHalla's approach skips keyframe images entirely. The prompts are written timelines: zero to two seconds, close-up; hard cut; two to three and a half, medium shot. TechHalla claims every cut landed. Those were seven- and eight-second clips, so it's unclear whether the same method holds at thirty.

So keyframes help. They don't guarantee every beat.

## 6. Where it breaks

**VO:**
Fast action without keyframes is where things come apart. Oprelia's rooftop fight: "the moment they do fast movements, it starts to break apart." Characters jump position between cuts. Oprelia retried: "another one that I deleted." Still distorted.

Length has its own failure. In a 14-second Pixar-style clip, a line got cut off mid-sentence. Oprelia's guess is that the clip ran out of time.

To be fair, not every fight failed. On X, Dani__oros posted a 30-second anime fight, writing that it "came out better than I thought it would, considering how vague my prompt was." The prompt wasn't shared, so we can't say why it worked.

And here's a harsher benchmark from Chinese creator kzhu, who runs the same pelican-on-a-bicycle prompt on every model. The list for Kling 4.0: a stray wall clock, a bike you never see whole, a glass bottle floating in the air, a mirror wall that reflects nothing, and a selfie taken with the wrong camera. kzhu confirms the model only by saying so, so treat this as their report.

And the models ran at different resolutions before everything was upscaled to 4K. One take per model doesn't measure a model. It's a snapshot.

## 8. Retakes and cost

**VO:**
Nobody published a retake rate for 30-second clips. What we have is anecdotes. Oprelia deleted attempts. ## 9. Should you switch? A decision framework (NON-TEST)

**VO:**
Here's how I'd read this if you make AI video for clients.

If it's a slow scene with one or two people, dialogue, or a presenter, try a single long generation. That's where the 30-second clips held up.

If it's action, use keyframes, or plan on short clips. The keyframed fight was the best result we saw. The unkeyed fights were the worst.

If consistency across many characters matters, references did the work in TechHalla's clips. But those clips were eight seconds.

And if precise timing matters, don't expect a timed prompt to hit every beat. Plan to cut.

One more thing. Budget for retakes as if nothing changed. Nobody has shown that a 30-second clip needs fewer attempts than three 10-second ones. That might turn out to be true. Right now it's untested. And until Kling publishes its credit rates, you can't compare the cost either.

This is my reading of a small set of early tests. It isn't a benchmark.
